Does the 4 a.m. session script actually predict the day?

A viral pitch claims the market writes its script overnight: if one session reverses, the next continues — a rotation so reliable it “has done this every single day for decades.” We tested every claim it makes against ten years of open-to-close data.

The claim, condensed
“If the previous session reversed, the next session continues. If Asia reversed, London continues. If London reversed, New York continues. If no session reversed, New York is the reversal.”
Entry model: trade New York in London’s direction.
Marketed result: 43% → 64% win rate · “$30k+/month” · DM me ‘SYSTEM’
Does not hold water

Zero of the four scenarios beat the base rate. Three fail in the opposite direction.

The impressive-looking hit rates are just market drift and global co-movement. Conditioning on a “reversal” adds no information — and often subtracts it.

The sleight of hand

Why 56% sounds like an edge and isn’t

The pitch quotes a conditional hit rate but never tells you the base rate. New York “continues” London most days no matter what came before, because world equity markets move together and drift up. That co-movement — not the “script” — is the whole number.

The kernel of truth

There is one real effect — and it’s the opposite of what’s being sold

The pitch’s most literal claim is that the candle printed while you slept tells you the day. Tested directly on the S&P 500 — does the overnight move predict the intraday open-to-close move? — there is a small, statistically real tilt. But it is momentum, not reversal, and it is a whisper, not a script.

S&P 500 · overnight → intraday · 2,515 days

When it gaps up overnight, the day continues up — 8.8 points more often.

Correlation of the overnight and intraday returns is +0.23. Sign agreement 54.9% (z = 4.89). This is genuine intraday momentum — documented in the peer-reviewed literature — but an 8.8-point tilt is a long way from “the market tells you the entire day’s direction.”

Note the direction: a gap that keeps going is continuation. The script sells “reversal” as the signal — the exact opposite of the only effect that survives.

What the research actually says

The literature nearby — and what it does and doesn’t support

Real academic work exists on cross-session effects. None of it endorses a deterministic reversal-continuation rotation. It documents weak statistical tilts and volatility spillovers — useful, but nothing you can run a “done by 10:30” system on.

Partly supports the kernel Market Intraday Momentum
Gao, Han, Li & Zhou · J. Financial Economics 129(2), 2018

The first half-hour return predicts the last half-hour return in S&P ETFs, 1993–2013. Real, but small, and strongest only on high-volume / high-volatility / news days — the momentum our Track 1 finds, not a reversal script.

Volatility, not direction Meteor Showers or Heat Waves?
Engle, Ito & Lin · Econometrica 58(3), 1990

Volatility spills from one time-zone’s market to the next (“meteor showers”). Foundational for cross-session linkage — but it’s about how much price moves, never which way. It cannot power a directional script.

Co-movement, not a script Correlations in Price Changes & Volatility across International Stock Markets
Hamao, Masulis & Ng · Rev. Financial Studies 3(2), 1990

Documents exactly the global co-movement that inflates the “continuation” base rates here. Markets follow each other — which is why NY “continues” London 58% of the time with no signal at all.

Cautions against it A Tug of War: Overnight versus Intraday Expected Returns
Lou, Polk & Skouras · J. Financial Economics 134(1), 2019

Overnight and intraday returns frequently carry opposite signs for the same strategies. Direct evidence that “the pre-market candle tells you the day” is unreliable as a rule — the relationship flips by strategy and horizon.

Methodology & honest limits

How a session was scored

Each market’s local cash session is one candle: sign(close − open). Within a calendar date the chain runs Asia → London → New York, which is the true chronological order (Tokyo morning, London midday, NY afternoon). A session continues if its sign equals the prior session’s, reverses if opposite.

Indices: Asia = Nikkei 225 ^N225 (robustness: ^HSI, ^KS11), London = FTSE 100 ^FTSE, US = S&P 500 ^GSPC. Common trading days only.

How each claim was judged

Every conditional rate is tested against its base rate — the unconditional continuation/reversal frequency — not against 50%, because drift and co-movement already push those well past a coin flip. Reported with Wilson 95% intervals and a two-sided z-test of the excess.

Track 1 is a cleaner single-instrument test: the S&P’s overnight leg (prev close→open) vs its intraday leg (open→close), which needs no cross-market proxy.

Where this is a proxy

The pitch trades one instrument (futures / FX / gold) across intraday sessions. Track 2 uses three different indices as session stand-ins — a reasonable, transparent proxy, but not the identical asset the story describes. Track 1 removes that gap and still finds only a weak momentum tilt.

The original claim is also partly unfalsifiable: “reversal” and “sweep” are discretionary chart labels. This audit picks the most reasonable objective definition; under it, the claim fails.

What would change the answer

A true test of the intraday version needs tick / hourly futures data segmented into Asia, London, and NY hours on one instrument — not available here. If the effect were as strong as advertised (“every single day for decades”), it would still show through the daily proxy. It does not.

Sample is a 2016–2026 bull market; drift flatters every “up continues up” count, which makes the failure to beat base rates more damning, not less.
